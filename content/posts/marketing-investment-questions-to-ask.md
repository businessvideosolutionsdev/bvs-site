Another marketing bill is a real decision for a local business. Before committing, you want to understand what the work includes, how it will be evaluated and what your own team needs to do.

Ben from [Southern Diesel Supply](/testimonials/southern-diesel-supply/) describes that hesitation in his BVS interview. He was concerned about another expense. Looking back, he reports a positive experience, but the video does not include an exact return or reporting period. His story starts a conversation; your plan still needs its own numbers and expectations.

Here are five questions that can make that conversation more useful.

## 1. Which service and customer are we trying to reach?

Name the work you want more of. Include the service area and any important fit criteria. A business with room for larger installations has a different need from one trying to fill short service appointments.

This gives the creative, targeting and follow-up a shared purpose. It also helps the team recognize whether the inquiries match the work you wanted.

## 2. What is included in the cost?

Ask for a clear breakdown of content creation, campaign management and the advertising spend paid to the platform. Confirm any setup work, recurring costs and responsibilities that sit with your team.

The purpose is to understand the complete scope. If you compare two plans, make sure you are comparing the same work and the same assumptions.

## 3. What will we count as a useful inquiry?

A form submission is only the beginning of the conversation. Decide what makes an inquiry relevant: the location, the requested service, the person's needs and whether the business can help.

Then agree how the team will record the next steps. A simple sequence can be enough to start: inquiry received, contact made, estimate or appointment arranged, and job won or lost. Keep the information consistent so campaign discussions are based on the same definitions.

## 4. Who owns the follow-up?

An ad can start a conversation, but someone still needs to answer it. Clarify who checks messages, returns calls and updates the record when an inquiry becomes a customer.

Tell your marketing team about capacity limits and changes in availability. Feedback about the actual conversations is useful when refining the message and deciding which services to emphasize.

## 5. When will we review the plan?

Agree on a review schedule and the questions you will discuss. Look at the inquiries, their fit, the work booked and the costs alongside one another. Ask what the team has learned and what it proposes to change next.

Avoid treating one client's result as a forecast for your business. Differences in the offer, market, budget and sales process matter. A useful plan makes its assumptions visible and gives you a way to revisit them.

## Bring context to the first call

You do not need a perfect dashboard. Bring what you know about recent inquiries, the jobs you want, your service area and the team's capacity. Our [strategy-call preparation guide](/blog/prepare-for-a-marketing-strategy-call/) can help organize those notes.
